# What the quotes are for

Pip's brief, 2026-08-30, in his own framing. This is the tone rule, and it is
what a candidate quote is judged against.

## The thesis

> This is hard. You're going to lose. We told you you were going to lose before
> Pip started making the game. And the real-world version of this is STILL
> harder than the game.

Call of Duty puts a quote about the difficulty of war on the death screen. It
is not mocking the player. It is telling them that losing here is not a
personal failure, because the thing itself is genuinely hard, and it has been
hard for everyone who ever tried it.

That is the effect being reached for: **the defiance that makes someone hit
respawn.**

## Who it is written for

**Players who are not safety-pilled.** Someone who picked this up as a
management game and lost, and does not already believe the underlying problem
is hard. They need to be told, by someone other than the game's author, that
trying and losing at this is the heroic thing rather than the embarrassing one.

A player who already agrees will enjoy the quote. A player who does not is the
one it is actually for.

## The constraint that keeps it honest

**A quote must work even if the game is bad.**

If a line only lands because the player was moved by the preceding twenty
minutes, it is doing the game's job rather than its own. These are real people
saying true things about a real problem, and they should be worth reading on
their own terms: on a death screen, in a screenshot, out of context, in a
version of this game that did not turn out very good.

This is also the honest position. The game is not finished and does not claim
to be. A quote that quietly asserts the game is profound is overselling in
exactly the way this estate has already had to retract once.

## First pass: difficulty only

Ruled 2026-08-30. **Collect difficulty-flavoured quotes. Steer well clear of
accountability.**

Accountability quotes come later in development and need much more careful
thought and guarding: see the Australian position in `README.md`, where
`Russell v ABC (No 3) [2023] FCA 1223` cost a national broadcaster over AUD
400,000 on one procedural failure. The schema supports that tier and the gate
enforces it. Nothing is collected into it yet, on purpose.

## What a good difficulty quote looks like

- It says the problem is hard, or subtle, or unsolved, or harder than it looks.
- It was said by someone who was **trying**, not by someone dismissing the
  attempt. Defiance, not despair, and never contempt for the effort.
- It predates the game, ideally by years. The point is that this was known
  before anyone made a game about it.
- It survives being read alone.

## What is not wanted, even where the licence permits

- Anything that reads as mocking the player for losing.
- Doom without agency. The player is being invited to try again, so a line
  whose only content is that it is hopeless works against the entire purpose.
- A quote whose force depends on knowing who said it and disliking them. That
  is the accountability tier wearing a difficulty costume.
- Anything about a named person rather than about the problem.

## Measured, so nobody repeats it: the easiest source is the worst one

The 1,129 arXiv abstracts in `data/raw/arxiv_abstracts/dumps/2026-08-29_231114/`
are **CC0, verified, already fetched, and need no permission at all**. They are
the obvious place to start and they do not work.

Scanned 2026-08-30 for difficulty language: 232 candidate sentences across 198
papers. Reading them, essentially none survive the brief. A representative
sample of the best of them:

> Neural networks are still hard to design.

> Quantifying predictive uncertainty in NNs is a challenging and yet unsolved
> problem.

> CNN representations are notoriously difficult for humans to interpret.

These are true, and they are about narrow technical subproblems rather than
about the thing being hard. Academic abstracts hedge by convention, and a
hedged sentence about a subproblem cannot tell a player that losing was not
their fault. **The licence was never the constraint. The prose is.**

The quotes that work will come from essays, talks, and forum posts, where
someone was writing to be understood rather than to be precise. Most of those
need permission, which is the actual cost of this feature and is worth paying.

Do not re-run the abstract scan expecting a different answer.

## Held back, and why: two licence-clear quotes with no archive

Two quotes are licence-clear and are NOT in the corpus, because the Wayback
Machine holds no snapshot of their source and a save request did not complete:

- Gwern Branwen, `https://gwern.net/mcts-ai`, CC0. "The bad solutions and
  actions are simple and predictable outcomes of the most straightforward easy
  approaches, and that it is the good solutions and actions which are hard to
  make the AIs reliably discover."
- Zvi Mowshowitz, `thezvi.wordpress.com/2023/05/01/the-rocket-alignment-problem-part-2/`.
  "If you create an AGI and fail at its alignment, you do not get to scrap the
  experiment, learn from what happened. You do not get to try, try again until
  you succeed, the way we do for things like rocket launches."

Both were verified verbatim. The gate requires an archive before anything is
servable, and it is right to: a personal site can change or vanish, and without
a capture we could not later show we quoted someone correctly. Retry the
capture and they can go straight in, no permission needed.

The second one is a notable near-miss for the brief. It is the best statement
of "no retries" in the whole set, on a death screen that exists to offer one.

## Permissions are part of the fun

Pip's view, and it matches what the sourcing research found: most people will
be somewhere between neutral and flattered to have something they wrote appear
in a game about the thing they care about. Asking is incremental, pleasant, and
for LessWrong and the Alignment Forum it is the only lawful route anyway.

So a candidate with `not_yet_asked` is not a failure. It is the normal state of
a quote that has been found and not yet asked about, and the corpus is expected
to hold many of them.
